Companion to Medieval and Renaissance Music

Tess Knighton and David Fallows (eds.) · E:\pdf\music\Knighton Fallows Companion to medieval and renaissance music libgen li.pdf

Edited by Tess Knighton and David Fallows and first published in Britain by Orion, this Schirmer Books companion (1992) is a multi-author reference collecting short specialist essays on medieval and Renaissance music (roughly 500-1400 and the fifteenth-sixteenth centuries respectively, per the Library of Congress subject headings on its copyright page). Structurally the book opens with a section titled 'The music of the past and the modern ear,' beginning with Daniel Leech-Wilkinson's essay 'The good, the bad and the boring' and James Haar's 'Value judgments in music of the Renaissance' — a self-aware framing choice that interrogates how modern listeners and scholars impose anachronistic aesthetic judgments on early repertoire before getting into the historical content itself. From there the book proceeds through essays by leading early-music scholars covering genres (plainchant, polyphony, the mass and motet, secular song traditions), regional traditions, notational practice, instruments, and major figures across the period, in the reference-companion format of short (2,000-5,000 word) contributions by different specialists rather than a single-author narrative history. It includes illustrations, music examples set by a professional music engraver, and a substantial bibliographic apparatus, aimed at undergraduate and graduate music students, performers of early music, and generally curious readers with some existing music-history background — it assumes familiarity with basic music-historical periodization and some notational literacy but explains specialist terms as needed. For SYNTHBUILDER this has no direct technical relevance — no synthesis, sound technology, or DSP content, and its subject matter (vocal polyphony, plainchant, Renaissance instrumental consort music) is stylistically and technologically about as distant from chiptune synthesis as the library gets. Its value to Ted is general music-historical breadth and possibly compositional inspiration (early music's modal harmony and non-functional voice-leading could be an interesting source of melodic/harmonic material to render in chiptune voices), but it is not a resource this project would consult for building synths or plugins.

The book's opening section — essays by Daniel Leech-Wilkinson and James Haar explicitly interrogating modern listeners' anachronistic value judgments about early music before any historical content is presented — is a notably self-aware editorial choice for a 1992 reference companion, anticipating a methodological concern (how do we avoid imposing present-day aesthetic categories on music from a fundamentally different sound-world) that recurs, in a very different register, in Andra Ivănescu's argument elsewhere in this library about "authentic" versus idealized "retro" chiptune sound. Both books, from opposite ends of music history, are ultimately asking the same question: what does it mean to accurately represent a historical sound practice to a modern audience that didn't grow up inside its assumptions, rather than smoothing it into something more immediately palatable. That parallel is conceptual, not technical — this companion has no bearing on synthesis, DSP, or NES sound-chip architecture, and its essay-collection format (short, specialist contributions on plainchant, polyphony, mass and motet traditions, regional traditions, and instruments, aimed at students and performers with existing music-historical grounding) makes it a reference work to consult on a specific topic rather than a book meant to be read start to finish. Its place in Ted's library is general music-historical breadth, with early music's modal, non-functional harmonic language offered here honestly as a distant compositional-inspiration possibility rather than anything the project's current docs pursue.

This summary is based on the book's stated editorial framing, its essay-collection structure, and its general subject coverage as given in its front matter, not a close reading of its individual specialist contributions.
