The Organ Preludes of Johann Sebastian Bach

George B. Stauffer · E:\pdf\music\Bach\[Studies in Musicology, 27] George B. Stauffer - The Organ Preludes of Johann Sebastian Bach (1980, UMI Research Press) - libgen.li.pdf

Part of the 'Studies in Musicology' monograph series edited by George Buelow at Indiana University (the extracted front matter lists neighboring volumes on topics like Beethoven's sketches, the Baroque guitar, and Marschner's stage works, situating this squarely as specialist academic musicology rather than a general-audience book), Stauffer's study focuses specifically on Bach's 'free' organ preludes — the toccatas, preludes, and fantasias not paired with a fugue in the familiar prelude-and-fugue format, or considered as a genre in their own right. The book traces the genre's stylistic evolution across Bach's career: his early exposure to North German organist-composers (the Buxtehude/Reincken/Böhm tradition of virtuosic, sectional, improvisatory-style organ writing centered on Lübeck and Hamburg), through his Weimar and Köthen years, to the more contrapuntally integrated and formally consolidated preludes of his Leipzig maturity. Typical of this era's source-critical musicology, the book works closely with manuscript and early-print evidence to establish chronology and authenticity, and offers formal/harmonic analysis of individual preludes to support its stylistic argument. It is written for organists, keyboard musicologists, and graduate students in historical musicology — it assumes score-reading fluency, familiarity with Baroque formal conventions, and interest in source chronology, not a general music-history readership. For SYNTHBUILDER this has no direct technical value — no synthesis, DSP, or sound-technology content of any kind, and its subject (solo pipe-organ repertoire) is stylistically about as far as possible from NES chiptune voices. Its relevance to Ted's broader practice is purely as deep Bach/keyboard-repertoire background, potentially useful if a future arrangement project wanted to adapt Bach's free-prelude keyboard textures (the toccata-like figuration, pedal-point sections, and sectional contrasts) into a chiptune or synth idiom, but that would be an indirect, creative leap rather than anything the book itself addresses.
