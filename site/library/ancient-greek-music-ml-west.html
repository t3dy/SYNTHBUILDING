Ancient Greek Music

Ancient Greek Music (Clarendon Press/Oxford, first published 1992, this a 1994 paperback issue) is the standard modern scholarly reconstruction of what is actually known — and how little directly survives — about ancient Greek music. Written by M. L. West, one of the twentieth century's most eminent classicists (renowned for work on Greek epic, lyric poetry, and Near Eastern influences on Greek literature), the book tackles a genuinely difficult evidentiary problem: almost no continuous notated Greek music survives, so reconstruction depends on piecing together scattered literary references (Plato and Aristotle's philosophical discussions of musical modes and their ethical/psychological effects, technical treatises like those of Aristoxenus), a small number of inscriptions and papyrus fragments preserving actual musical notation, depictions of instruments in vase painting and sculpture, and surviving instrument fragments themselves (auloi, lyres, kitharas). West systematically works through the surviving notational system (a letter-based notation distinct from and much older than modern staff notation), the Greek theoretical understanding of scales, modes (harmoniai), and tuning systems (the tetrachord as the basic building block, various genera — diatonic, chromatic, enharmonic), rhythm and meter's relationship to poetic meter, instruments and their construction/tuning, and the few substantial surviving musical fragments (such as the Delphic Hymns and the Seikilos epitaph), offering his own transcriptions into modern notation where the evidence allows. It is written for classicists, musicologists, and advanced students with some background in Greek (quotations are often given in the original) and music theory; it is not an accessible introduction but a rigorous scholarly reconstruction. For SYNTHBUILDER this book has no direct technical relevance — no synthesis, DSP, or modern sound technology content — but its deep engagement with alternative tuning systems, modal theory, and the tetrachord-based Greek scalar system is a genuinely interesting resource if SYNTHBUILDER or a sibling project ever wanted to implement historically informed microtonal or non-equal-tempered tuning tables in a JSFX synth, a technically feasible and musically distinctive direction the project's docs do not currently pursue.

West's reputation as one of the twentieth century's most eminent classicists — known well beyond ancient music specifically for work connecting Greek epic and lyric poetry to Near Eastern literary traditions — lends this book unusual authority within its narrow field; it remains, decades after first publication, the standard English-language reference specifically because the evidentiary problem it tackles (reconstructing a musical practice from scattered literary references, a handful of inscriptions and papyrus fragments, and instrument remains, with almost no continuous notated music surviving at all) has not been substantially reopened by later scholarship in a way that displaced it. The specific technical apparatus West works through — the tetrachord as the basic scalar building block, the three genera (diatonic, chromatic, enharmonic) that could each fill a tetrachord differently, and the harmoniai (modes) built from combinations of tetrachords — describes a tuning and scale system fundamentally unlike the twelve-tone equal temperament every other technical resource in this library (Cann, Pirkle, Yelton, Friedman) assumes without comment. That difference is exactly what makes the book a genuine, if currently unused, resource for SYNTHBUILDER: a JSFX synth's pitch table is just an array of frequency values, and nothing in the JSFX language itself requires equal temperament — implementing a historically accurate ancient Greek tetrachordal tuning table, or transcribing one of the few surviving fragments West discusses (the Delphic Hymns, the Seikilos epitaph) into a chiptune voice, would be a technically modest but conceptually distinctive practice exercise entirely outside anything the project's current docs describe.

This summary is based on the book's stated scope, its documented evidentiary method, and West's broader scholarly standing, not a close reading of its individual technical arguments about specific surviving fragments.
