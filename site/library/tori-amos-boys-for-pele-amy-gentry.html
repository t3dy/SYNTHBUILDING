Tori Amos's Boys for Pele (33 1/3 series)

Amy Gentry · E:\pdf\music\Amy Gentry Tori Amos s Boys for Pele Bloomsbury Academic.pdf

Amy Gentry's 33 1/3 entry covers Tori Amos's third solo studio album, Boys for Pele (1996), notable both for being Amos's first self-produced record (after working with outside producers on her earlier albums) and for its unusual instrumentation choices — extensive use of harpsichord and a Bösendorfer Imperial piano alongside her more familiar piano-and-voice core, recorded partly in a deconsecrated Irish church, contributing to the record's stark, intense, sometimes confrontational sound. The book likely engages with Amos's assertion of full creative and production control at a moment when few women in her commercial position had it, the album's dense mythological and religious imagery (its title referencing the Hawaiian volcano goddess Pele), and its critical reception as either a masterpiece or a difficult, alienating listen depending on the critic — consistent with the 33 1/3 format's mix of criticism, biography, and production history. It is written for readers already invested in 1990s alternative/art-pop and feminist music criticism, assumes no technical music-production background, and offers criticism rather than how-to instruction, though its attention to Amos's self-production and specific keyboard-instrument choices (harpsichord vs. piano vs. Bösendorfer) gives it slightly more instrument-specific substance than some other entries in the series. For SYNTHBUILDER this book has no direct technical relevance — no synthesis, DSP, or sound-design content — and its subject (acoustic piano/harpsichord-centered singer-songwriter production) is stylistically unrelated to chiptune or synthesizer programming. Its place in Ted's library is purely as music-criticism/personal-taste reading alongside the collection's other 33 1/3 titles, not as a resource this project would consult.

Amos's decision to self-produce Boys for Pele after working with outside producers on her earlier records is worth underlining as a genuine instance of an artist asserting full technical and creative control at a moment when few women in her commercial position held it — a story with a loose thematic echo in Anwen Crawford's account of Courtney Love's authorship being persistently doubted on Hole's Live Through This, also in this library, even though the two records sound nothing alike and Amos's self-production was never seriously questioned the way Love's songwriting credit was. The specific instrumentation choices Gentry's book likely covers — extensive harpsichord and a Bösendorfer Imperial piano used alongside Amos's more familiar core piano-and-voice sound, tracked partly in a deconsecrated Irish church for its acoustic character — give this entry slightly more concrete instrument- and space-specific detail than some of the other 33 1/3 volumes in this collection, closer in that narrow respect to the production-attentive Homogenic and Hounds of Love entries than to the more purely lyrical/biographical titles. Boys for Pele's reception split sharply between critics who read its harpsichord-and-church-recorded intensity as Amos's most fully realized artistic statement and those who found it needlessly difficult relative to her more approachable earlier work -- a split Gentry's volume, like most 33 1/3 entries, likely takes as its critical occasion rather than resolving one way or the other. That kind of divided critical reception recurs across several of the women-led 1990s alternative-rock records covered in this library's 33 1/3 cluster (Love's authorship doubted on Live Through This, Babes in Toyland's gender politics on Fontanelle), suggesting the pattern is as much about how the era's critical establishment received assertive women artists as about any one record's specific merits.
