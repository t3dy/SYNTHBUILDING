Selena Chambers's entry in the 33 1/3 series covers Fontanelle (1992), the second full-length album by the Minneapolis all-female punk/noise-rock trio Babes in Toyland, released on Reprise after the band's earlier Twin/Tone and Sub Pop-adjacent work, and produced with input from Lee Ranaldo of Sonic Youth. Consistent with the 33 1/3 format, expect a blend of album-specific criticism (close attention to specific songs, the band's guitar/bass/drums sound and Kat Bjelland's distinctive vocal delivery), band and scene history (the Minneapolis alternative/punk scene, the band's relationship to the contemporaneous riot grrrl movement despite some ambivalence about being categorized within it), and the gender politics surrounding an aggressively loud all-women rock band navigating a male-dominated grunge-era major-label landscape. The book is written for readers invested in 1990s alternative/punk history and feminist music criticism, assuming no technical music background and offering no production or synthesis instruction — it is squarely cultural/music criticism. For SYNTHBUILDER this has no technical relevance whatsoever — no synthesis, DSP, or production content — and its subject matter (raw guitar-bass-drums noise rock) is stylistically unrelated to chiptune or synthesizer work. Its place in Ted's library and on a music showcase page is purely as evidence of broad, personally significant listening and critical interest, alongside the other 33 1/3 titles, not as a resource this project would draw on.

Produced with input from Lee Ranaldo of Sonic Youth, Fontanelle sits at an interesting production crossroads — a major-label budget (Reprise) applied to a deliberately abrasive, largely unpolished noise-rock sound, guided by a producer-adjacent figure himself best known for guitar-noise experimentation rather than commercial mainstream production — which the book likely addresses as part of its account of the record's making, alongside Kat Bjelland's distinctive vocal approach and the band's ambivalent relationship to the riot grrrl label frequently applied to them despite the band's own reported resistance to that framing. None of this bears on SYNTHBUILDER in any technical sense: raw guitar-bass-drums noise rock, recorded to sound deliberately unpolished, shares no instrumentation, production technique, or sound-design concern with chiptune synthesis, and this book belongs on a music-showcase page purely as evidence of Ted's broader listening range and his interest in 1990s alternative and punk history and its gender politics, alongside Anwen Crawford's book on Hole and Ronen Givony's on Jawbreaker — together these three form a loose cluster of 33 1/3 titles specifically about 1990s American underground rock's relationship to gender, scene politics, and major-label ambivalence, distinct from the more production-technology-focused Devo and Kate Bush entries elsewhere on the same shelf.

This summary is based on the album's documented production context and the book's stated critical scope, not a page-by-page reading of Chambers's specific arguments.

Fontanelle's place in the band's discography also matters for context: it followed the more Twin/Tone- and Sub Pop-adjacent early records and marked the band's major-label arrival on Reprise, a trajectory shared in broad outline by several other acts covered in this library's 33 1/3 titles moving from independent to major-label distribution in the same stretch of years (Hole's Geffen-distributed Live Through This among them) -- a pattern of the early-1990s alternative-rock gold rush worth naming across these entries rather than treating each as an isolated case.
