The Rock Synthesizer Manual: A Revised Guide for the Electronic Musician

Geary Yelton · E:\pdf\music\Geary Yelton The Rock Synthesizer Manual_ A Revised Guide for the Electronic Musician Rock Technology Pubns.pdf

Published by Rock Tech Publications in 1984, The Rock Synthesizer Manual is a period document of early-1980s analog/voltage-controlled synthesizer practice, written specifically for rock and pop musicians (rather than academic electronic-music composers) who needed to understand the generation of standalone hardware synths — Moog, ARP, Oberheim, Roland, Sequential Circuits-era instruments — that had become central to the era's music. Its table of contents runs through 'Electronic Sound' fundamentals in a logical build-up: basics of sound (waveform, frequency, amplitude), electronic sound sources (oscillators), voltage control (the defining paradigm of the era's analog synths, where every parameter is controlled by a variable voltage rather than digital data), voltage-controlled amplifiers (VCAs), envelope generation, filtering, modulation, keyboard controllers, sequencers, sample-and-hold circuits (a classic source of the era's 'random arpeggio' textures), footpedal controllers and footswitches, and programmable patch memory (a genuinely new feature at the time, replacing patch cables/knob-recall with digitally stored parameter presets). The book teaches the voltage-control paradigm directly rather than through any particular manufacturer's synth, in the tradition of period primers aimed at giving working musicians (not engineers) a functional map of what each module does and how to combine them into usable sounds. It predates MIDI's full dominance and is squarely a hardware-modular/voltage-control-era text rather than a software or DAW-plugin one. For SYNTHBUILDER, this book is directly useful as core subtractive-synthesis conceptual grounding — VCO/VCF/VCA/envelope/LFO signal flow is exactly the architecture a JSFX subtractive synth needs to implement, and the book explains each stage's musical function clearly and concisely without assuming DSP programming knowledge. It won't show code, but as a plain-language explanation of what a filter cutoff or an envelope's decay stage is supposed to do to a sound, it pairs well with Cann's more patch-oriented book and Pirkle's DSP-implementation book, filling the conceptual middle ground between the two.

Medium-High — a clear, non-technical explanation of the VCO/VCF/VCA/envelope/LFO subtractive-synthesis signal chain that any JSFX subtractive synth needs to implement, though it teaches concepts, not code.
