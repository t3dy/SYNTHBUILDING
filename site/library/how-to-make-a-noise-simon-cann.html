How To Make A Noise: A Comprehensive Guide to Synthesizer Programming

Simon Cann · E:\pdf\music\Cann Simon How to make a noise _ a comprehensive guide to synthesizer programming Coombe Hill.pdf

How To Make A Noise is a practitioner's synthesis primer built entirely around making usable sounds rather than abstract signal theory. Cann (a working sound designer) structures the book as a sequence of twelve chapters plus four 'refill' supplements: Getting Started, Envelopes, Filters, Sound Sources, Modulation (concept, then in-practice), Frequency Modulation Synthesis (with a Surge-specific supplement), Wave-Sequencing, Additive Synthesis, FX, Putting It All Together, and Building Patches, followed by refills on arpeggiators/step sequencers, 'pushing the sonic envelope,' and synthetic drum/percussion design. Each concept — ADSR vs. DAHDSR vs. DASSDSR envelope shapes, low-pass/high-pass/band-pass/notch/formant/comb filter types, basic wave shapes (sine, sawtooth, square/pulse, triangle, noise) and their combination via doubling, layering, and hard sync — is explained and then immediately demonstrated as an audible patch on one of six late-2000s/2010s software synths: Camel Audio Cameleon 5000, Big Tick Rhino, Vember Audio Surge, reFX Vanguard, Wusik Wusikstation, and Cakewalk Z3TA+. The FM chapter is unusually thorough for a 'basics' book: it covers operator/carrier-modulator relationships, 1:1 vs. varying ratios, parallel vs. cascading operators, and building a first FM patch from scratch, essentially teaching the same conceptual territory as Yamaha DX7-style synthesis without requiring DX7 hardware. The additive chapter honestly flags 'Warning!! Mathematics Ahead' before walking through building square, sawtooth, and triangle waves from summed sine partials — a direct, worked illustration of Fourier synthesis. The book is written for musicians and producers who own a software synth and want to move past factory presets to sound design they understand, not for DSP engineers; there is no code, no math beyond arithmetic ratios, and no discussion of how any of this is implemented in a signal-processing sense. It is squarely a 'what knob does what, and why does it sound like that' book, organized so a reader can skip to the synthesis method they need (subtractive vs. FM vs. additive vs. wave-sequencing) independently. For SYNTHBUILDER, this is high-value conceptual scaffolding rather than implementation help: it will not show how to write a JSFX oscillator or filter, but it gives a working vocabulary and mental model — what an ADSR stage should sound like, what a resonant low-pass sweep should sound like, how FM operator ratios generate inharmonic vs. harmonic spectra — that is exactly what's needed to know whether a hand-written JSFX synth 'sounds right' before worrying about whether it is DSP-correct. It is a good source of target patches (basses, leads, pads, plucks, synthetic kick/snare/hats) to try to reproduce once a JSFX synth has its first audible note, per the project's own 'one audible note before building anything else' rule.

Relevance to SYNTHBUILDER
High — the clearest general synthesis-concepts primer in the library (oscillators/envelopes/filters/FM/additive/modulation), though it teaches ears-and-patches, not DSP code or JSFX specifics.
