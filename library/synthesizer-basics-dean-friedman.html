Synthesizer Basics

Dean Friedman · E:\pdf\music\Dean Friedman Synthesizer Basics Amsco Music.pdf

Synthesizer Basics is an image-scanned, entry-level instructional book billed on its cover as 'the definitive beginner's guide' — 'Tells you everything you need to know to really understand, program and start using your synthesizer. Also contains special sections on the Yamaha DX7, Sampling Devices and MIDI.' Written by Dean Friedman (better known as a singer-songwriter, e.g. 'Ariel' and 'Lucky Stars,' who also had a serious technical interest in synthesis and music technology journalism), the book dates to the mid-1980s heyday of both analog/hybrid subtractive synthesis and the newly dominant FM synthesis of Yamaha's DX7 — the cover's collage of keyboard and panel-slider photography is drawn entirely from period hardware (DX7-era sliders, membrane panels, and LCD displays are visible), situating the book precisely at the transition moment between knob-per-function analog synths and menu-diving digital FM synths. Its explicit coverage of three distinct areas — general synthesizer fundamentals (oscillators, envelopes, filters, modulation, in the analog/subtractive vocabulary standard to the era), the DX7 specifically (FM operator-based synthesis, at the time a genuinely new and confusing paradigm for most musicians), sampling devices (early hardware samplers), and the then-brand-new MIDI protocol — makes it a useful time-capsule of exactly what a working musician needed to learn in 1985-86 to use the generation of gear that dominated pop, film, and video-game (including early NES-adjacent) music production. It is written for musicians and hobbyists with no electronics or programming background, in the plain, non-technical 'basics' tradition typical of Amsco's instructional series. For SYNTHBUILDER, this book's chief value is historical/conceptual: it explains FM synthesis and MIDI from the exact era and mindset that also produced the NES's own sound hardware and the early-digital-synth sound SYNTHBUILDER is chasing, and its DX7/FM section is a good plain-language companion to the more code-focused FM material in Simon Cann's book. It teaches no DSP math or programming, but as an accessible period-accurate primer on 'what FM synthesis actually is and why MIDI exists,' it is a reasonable on-ramp before tackling JSFX implementation.

Dean Friedman's own biography adds a layer of interest here: better known to most readers as the singer-songwriter behind "Ariel" and "Lucky Stars," he was also a serious, hands-on synthesist and music-technology writer, and a musician writing a synthesizer primer for other working musicians (rather than an engineer writing for other engineers) shapes the whole tone of the book — it explains what a parameter does to the sound and why a player would want that, not how the circuit or algorithm behind it works. Positioned in the mid-1980s, the book captures a genuinely transitional moment that mirrors, in miniature, the technological transition SYNTHBUILDER itself is built around: just as the NES's 2A03 chip forced composers of the same era to write music within a small number of fixed pulse/triangle/noise channels, the DX7's FM synthesis forced 1980s musicians to learn an entirely new, non-intuitive operator-based sound-design paradigm that displaced the familiar VCO/VCF/VCA workflow almost overnight — both are stories of composers adapting creative practice to hard technical constraints, which is the same story NSFRIPPER's and GlitchMario's documented "blunders" tell about trying to reverse-engineer that constraint set decades later. The book's MIDI section is also worth noting for its historical position: MIDI was brand-new when this was written, and a plain-language explanation of why the protocol exists at all, written at the moment it was still a novelty rather than an assumed universal, is a useful companion to any modern JSFX work that takes MIDI input entirely for granted.

This summary is drawn from the book's stated cover description, its three-part scope (fundamentals, DX7, sampling/MIDI), and its historical positioning, not from a page-by-page reading of its instructional content.
