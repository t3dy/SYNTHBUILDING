The Second Sense: Studies in Hearing and Musical Judgement from Antiquity to the Seventeenth Century is Warburg Institute Surveys and Texts volume XXII, edited by Charles Burnett, Michael Fend, and Penelope Gouk, published by the Warburg Institute, University of London — a scanned 262-page academic essay collection confirmed by title page and full table of contents. The book is organized in two parts. Part I, 'Sound and Hearing,' moves chronologically and thematically through the philosophy and physiology of auditory perception: Alan Towey on Aristotle and Alexander of Aphrodisias's account of hearing and instantaneous change; Elizabeth Sears on the iconography of auditory perception in early medieval psalm illustration and exegesis; Charles Burnett himself on 'Sound and its Perception in the Middle Ages'; Thomas Frangenberg on Charles de Bovelles's Renaissance comparison of hearing and vision (Auditus visu prestantior — 'hearing more excellent than sight'); Penelope Gouk on English theories of hearing in the seventeenth century before and after Descartes; and Paolo Gozza on a mechanical account of hearing from the 'Galilean school,' focused on Pietro Mengoli's 1670 Speculationi di Musica. Part II, 'Musical Judgement,' covers Andrew Barker on Plato and Aristoxenus on the nature of melos (melody/pitch-motion); John Caldwell on the concept of musical judgment in Late Antiquity; Klaus-Jürgen Sachs on Boethius and 'the judgement of the ears' as a hidden challenge running through medieval and Renaissance music theory; and Michael Fend on the changing functions of senso and ragione (sense and reason) in late-sixteenth-century Italian music theory. A select bibliography and two indices (nominum, rerum) close the volume. This is rigorous history-of-science/history-of-ideas scholarship, written for historians of philosophy, science, and music theory — it assumes graduate-level familiarity with the relevant primary sources (often quoted in Latin/Greek/Italian) and offers no accessible summary for general readers. For SYNTHBUILDER this has no direct technical relevance — it concerns the history of theories about hearing and judgment, not acoustics-as-physics or sound-engineering practice, and contains no synthesis or DSP content. Its interest is as deep intellectual-history background on how 'hearing' itself has been theorized, tangential at best to a practical REAPER/JSFX project.

The book's two-part structure is worth laying out in a little more detail than the essay-by-essay list above, because it clarifies what kind of intellectual history this actually is: Part I traces the physiology and philosophy of hearing itself — from Aristotle and Alexander of Aphrodisias's account of instantaneous auditory change, through medieval and Renaissance treatments (Sears on psalm-illustration iconography, Burnett on medieval sound-perception, Frangenberg on de Bovelles's Renaissance comparison of hearing to sight), to the mechanical, proto-scientific accounts emerging from the "Galilean school" in the seventeenth century (Gouk on English post-Cartesian hearing theory, Gozza on Mengoli's 1670 mechanical acoustics) — while Part II turns from the physiology of hearing to the separate but related question of musical judgment: how listeners across the same span of centuries claimed to know good music from bad, from Plato and Aristoxenus's account of melos through Boethius's "judgement of the ears" (Sachs) to changing Italian Renaissance ideas about sense versus reason in musical taste (Fend). That trajectory — from "how does hearing physically work" to "how do we judge what we hear" — is a genuine, coherent intellectual arc, published as Warburg Institute Surveys and Texts volume XXII, one of a long-running series of rigorous, primary-source-grounded studies from an institution specifically dedicated to the history of classical tradition's survival into later European thought. None of it bears on modern acoustics-as-engineering or DSP, and SYNTHBUILDER's practical JSFX work will draw nothing from it directly, but it stands as the most rigorous historical treatment in this library of the underlying philosophical question — what does it mean to hear, and to judge what one hears — that every ear-test decision the project's own docs describe (the unmade NSFRIPPER A/B/C listening session, NESjamtools' "awaiting your ears" bug) implicitly depends on answering, however unphilosophically.

This summary is based on the book's confirmed table of contents and named contributors, not a close reading of each individual essay's argument.
