They Might Be Giants' Flood (33 1/3 series)

Philip Sandifer and S. Alexander Reed · E:\pdf\music\Sandifer Philip_ Reed S Alexander They Might Be Giants Flood Bloomsbury Academic.pdf

This 33 1/3 entry covers They Might Be Giants' third studio album, Flood (1990), the record that brought the Brooklyn duo (John Flansburgh and John Linnell) to mainstream alternative-rock attention via singles like 'Birdhouse in Your Soul' and 'Istanbul (Not Constantinople).' Co-authored by Philip Sandifer and S. Alexander Reed — Reed in particular has other academic writing on electronic and industrial music, giving this entry likely more attention to the album's actual production and instrumentation choices than a purely journalistic account might offer. Flood is notable for its dense, genre-promiscuous songwriting (drawing on 1950s pop, polka, children's music, and new-wave synth-pop within a single record) and its production, which blended live band instrumentation with era-typical drum machines, sequencers, and accordion as a lead melodic voice — an unusual combination that became central to the band's identity. As with other 33 1/3 volumes, expect track-by-track or thematic critical analysis combined with the album's recording history and cultural reception, written for informed fans and critics, assuming no formal music-theory or production background from the reader, and offering criticism/history rather than technical instruction. For SYNTHBUILDER this book offers no direct technical content — no synthesis, DSP, or plugin material — but its subject (a record built on hybrid drum-machine/sequencer-plus-acoustic-instrument arrangements, made by two songwriters with a strong interest in odd, playful sound-and-genre juxtaposition) is a reasonable aesthetic touchstone for anyone thinking about how to make chiptune or synthetic voices feel purposeful and musically integrated rather than merely technically accurate, but that connection is inspirational rather than instructional.

Co-author S. Alexander Reed's other academic work on electronic and industrial music is worth naming specifically here, since it distinguishes this entry from most of the other 33 1/3 titles in Ted's library: a critic with a documented scholarly interest in synthesizer-driven and programmed music is more likely to give Flood's drum-machine-and-accordion production real technical attention than a purely journalistic account of the band's lyrics and reception would, which puts this book closer in spirit (though not in technical depth) to the Devo and Kate Bush 33 1/3 entries than to the more purely biographical or scene-historical volumes on this shelf (Jawbreaker, Babes in Toyland, Hole). Flood's genre-promiscuous songwriting — pulling from 1950s pop, polka, children's music, and new-wave synth-pop within a single record — and its hybrid arrangement of live band instrumentation with drum machines, sequencers, and accordion as a genuine lead melodic voice is a real, if unusual, example of treating a "non-standard" timbre (accordion, in this case, rather than a synthesizer) as seriously as a conventional lead instrument, which is loosely the same design question SYNTHBUILDER's NES-voice work asks about chiptune tones: can a constrained or unusual sound source carry real musical weight rather than functioning as a novelty layer. That parallel remains inspirational rather than technical — nothing in this book teaches synthesis, DSP, or JSFX programming.

This summary is based on the album's well-documented production and instrumentation choices and the co-authors' stated backgrounds, not a page-by-page reading of Sandifer and Reed's specific critical arguments.
