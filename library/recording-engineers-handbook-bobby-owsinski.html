The Recording Engineer's Handbook, Second Edition

The Recording Engineer's Handbook (2nd edition, Course Technology/Cengage, 2009) is a comprehensively practical, chapter-by-chapter reference for the craft of audio recording, organized to mirror an actual session workflow. Chapter 1, 'How Microphones Work,' covers dynamic, ribbon, and condenser microphone designs, condenser myths and operational hints, phantom power, and the full vocabulary of mic specifications (sensitivity, overload characteristics, frequency response, self-noise, polar patterns, proximity effect) plus specialty mics (shotgun, lavalier, PZM, wireless, stereo, parabolic) and accessories (pop filters, windscreens, shock mounts). Chapter 2 surveys named classic and modern microphone models (Neumann U47/U67/M49/KM84/U87, AKG C12/C451/414, Shure SM57, Sennheiser MD421/441, Electro-Voice RE20, and current affordable equivalents) with interview material from mic designer David Bock. Chapter 3 covers basic recording equipment — mic preamps (vintage vs. modern, setup, direct injection) and compressor/limiters. Chapter 4, 'DAW Recording,' is the book's most directly relevant chapter to SYNTHBUILDER's domain: getting sound into a computer, how ADCs and DACs work, latency, FireWire-vs-USB tradeoffs, and headroom/gain-staging, including an interview with a Digidesign product manager. Later chapters cover stereo miking techniques (coincident, spaced, Decca Tree, near-coincident, baffled-omni pairs), multichannel tracking and phase-checking (by ear, oscilloscope plug-in, or phase meter), drum-kit preparation and miking (with an interview with 'Drum Doctor' Ross Garfield), and individual-instrument miking technique across a wide instrument list. The book is written for working and aspiring recording/mix engineers — assumes no DSP or programming background, is entirely equipment- and technique-focused rather than theoretical, and teaches craft judgment (mic choice, placement, gain staging) rather than synthesis or plugin construction. For SYNTHBUILDER, this is moderately useful adjacent knowledge: the DAW-recording chapter's coverage of latency, ADC/DAC behavior, and headroom/gain-staging is directly relevant to getting clean signal in and out of REAPER when testing a JSFX synth, and the general audio-engineering vocabulary (signal chain, gain staging, monitoring) is useful context — but the book does not address synthesis, DSP coding, or plugin formats (JSFX/VST3) at all, so its use is as supporting audio-engineering literacy rather than a synth-building resource.
