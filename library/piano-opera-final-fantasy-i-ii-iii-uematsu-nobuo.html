Piano Opera: Final Fantasy I II III (Sheet Music)

Nobuo Uematsu (composer); arr. Hiroyuki Nakayama · E:\pdf\music\Uematsu Nobuo Piano Opera Final Fantasy I II III Sheet Music.pdf

This is an image-scanned sheet-music collection (72 pages) rather than an expository or analytical text: a Japanese-language 'Piano Opera' piano-arrangement songbook of themes from the first three Final Fantasy games — all three originally released for the Famicom/NES between 1987 and 1990, meaning the source material was composed by Nobuo Uematsu within the exact 2A03 sound-chip constraints (square/pulse/triangle/noise channels, no true polyphonic sampling) that SYNTHBUILDER's NES-tone-accurate chiptune goal targets directly. The scanned staff-profile page identifies Nobuo Uematsu as composer — credited with, among many other things, the Final Fantasy VIII theme 'Eyes on Me' (Japan Gold Disc Award, 1999) and recognition in Time magazine's 'Time 100: The Next Wave — Music' innovators feature (2001) and Newsweek's list of 'people the world respects' from Japan (2007) — and Hiroyuki Nakayama (a Tokyo University of the Arts composition graduate) as the arranger and performer responsible for adapting Uematsu's original chiptune melodies and harmonic material into idiomatic solo-piano arrangements for this collection, plus his other orchestral/arrangement credits (Blue Dragon, Lost Odyssey, the Final Fantasy III DS remake). The book itself is purely notational content — piano scores with no prose commentary — meant for pianists to perform Uematsu's melodies away from their original chiptune voicing. For SYNTHBUILDER, this has real but indirect value: it is not a synthesis or DSP resource, but it is primary melodic/harmonic material composed specifically for NES sound-chip constraints by one of the most significant composers to work within them, making it a legitimate source of target melodies to re-synthesize accurately once a JSFX chiptune voice is working — testing whether a hand-built 2A03-emulating synth can faithfully reproduce (or interestingly reinterpret) canonical NES-era game music, and a useful cross-check against Uematsu's own harmonic language when deciding how period-accurate an arrangement should sound.
