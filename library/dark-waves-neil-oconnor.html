Dark Waves: The Synthesizer and the Dystopian Sound of Britain (1977-80)

Neil O'Connor · E:\pdf\music\Neil O Connor Dark Waves_ The Synthesizer and the Dystopian Sound of Britain Rowman Littlefield Publishers.pdf

Dark Waves is a scholarly cultural-history monograph in Rowman & Littlefield's 'Popular Musics Matter: Social, Political and Cultural Interventions' series (which also includes books on Joy Division, punk/post-punk deindustrialization in Manchester/Düsseldorf/Torino/Tampere, and Bob Marley's media representation, per the volume's own series list — situating this firmly as socio-politically engaged popular-music scholarship rather than a technical synthesis guide). Neil O'Connor's argument is that the synthesizer, as it entered British popular music in the late 1970s (via post-punk, early synth-pop, and industrial-adjacent acts), was heard and deployed specifically as a signifier of dystopia, alienation, and technological anxiety — its 'inhuman,' non-organic timbre mapped culturally onto a Britain undergoing deindustrialization, Cold War tension, and the social dislocations of the Thatcher-era transition (the book's 1977-80 window captures the moment just before Thatcher's 1979 election and its immediate aftermath). The book is structured with a section explicitly titled 'Background Noise' establishing this social and technological context, before presumably moving into closer case studies of specific artists, records, and synthesizer technologies (Korg, Roland, ARP, and similar instruments becoming newly affordable to working musicians in this period) that exemplify the 'dark wave' sound. It is written for readers interested in popular-music studies, cultural history, and post-punk/synth-pop scholarship — assumes interest in critical/cultural-studies framing of music rather than technical synthesis knowledge, and offers sustained argument rather than reference-style coverage. For SYNTHBUILDER, this book is aesthetic/historical rather than technical: it will not teach synthesis or DSP, but its close attention to what specific synthesizer timbres culturally signify (alienation, dystopia, technological unease) is a genuinely useful frame for thinking about the expressive and connotative possibilities of chiptune and synth voices beyond pure NES-accuracy — useful if SYNTHBUILDER's later, more expressive phase (post 'first audible note') wants to reason about what a given synth patch should mean, not just how it should measure.
